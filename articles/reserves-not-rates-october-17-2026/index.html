Reserves, Not Rates | October 17, 2026

Reserves, not rates.

SOFR printed 3.88% this week.

The target range is 3.75 to 4.00. So the most important overnight rate in the dollar system sits inside the band, toward the top of it, and nothing looks broken.

Look one layer down. The Fed’s overnight reverse repo facility, the sponge that parked trillions in idle cash through 2022 and 2023, took in about $350 million on October 1. For practical purposes it is empty. When reserves were abundant, excess cash sat in that facility and the funds rate hugged the bottom of the range. Now there is nothing left to park, and the rate hugs the top.

That is the signature of a system moving toward reserve scarcity while QT keeps draining. September 30 passed without drama: SOFR fixed at 3.90, up two basis points, on about $3.23 trillion of volume. The plumbing handled it, helped by sponsored repo netting, dealer inventories near local lows, and money funds still supplying cash.

The Fed’s own framework anticipates this moment. Its guidelines permit purchases of Treasury bills to keep reserves ample, and commentary this week notes the Desk has already had to work the plumbing. The October 27-28 meeting will be read for two decisions, not one: the rate, where markets price roughly an 86% chance of a hold per CME FedWatch on October 16, and the balance sheet, where the question is whether QT gets its end date.

Our read: the policy rate is the headline and reserves are the mechanism. The overnight rate is a quantity problem before it is a price problem. Every quarter-end, every tax date, every heavy issuance week tests whether intermediation still clears at these reserve levels.

Unconfirmed: desk chatter this week cites a mid-week Standing Repo Facility draw and a 29-basis-point SOFR-RRP gap. Neither prints on the New York Fed’s tape. Noise until confirmed.
