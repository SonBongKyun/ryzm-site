RYZM Framework Note | How US rates and dollar funding reach Korea | November 20, 2026

Sources checked November 20, 2026 at 20:30 KST.

This note maps how four things connect: US rates, dollar funding, the won and Korean assets. It is a reading framework. It is not a forecast, a trade signal or a view on where any price goes next.

The chain in one line

US policy rates set the price of dollars. Funding markets decide what it costs, and how easy it is, to get those dollars for a given term. USD/KRW is where Korea’s dollar supply and demand meet. Korean stocks and Korean government bonds (KTBs) are where foreign money comes in or goes out. A move in any one of these can start in any of the others, so the job is to find where it started, not to assume it.

1. Rates: the gap is an input, not a verdict

On October 28 the Federal Reserve kept the federal funds target range at 3.75% to 4.00% and the interest rate on reserve balances (IORB) at 3.90% (Fed implementation note). On October 22 the Bank of Korea held its base rate at 3.00%, after raising it in July and August (BOK October minutes).

The policy-rate gap matters mostly through hedging. Covered interest parity ties the USD/KRW forward rate to the interest-rate gap for the same maturity. So a won-based investor who hedges a dollar asset pays, or earns, roughly that gap, plus or minus the cross-currency basis. Our FX hedging reference guide works through the formula. The point here: a wider or narrower gap changes what hedged returns look like on both sides. It does not, on its own, decide which way money flows.

2. Funding: the price of dollars versus access to them

Funding is the second layer. In the US, overnight dollar rates such as SOFR, compared with IORB, show whether cash is tight inside the system (SOFR vs IORB guide). In Korea, the same pressure shows up in the FX swap market. A short-dated swap basis that moves on its own, with no change in rates, points to demand for dollars over a set term, not to a change in policy.

Korea has a public channel that sits between these markets. The foreign-exchange authorities and the National Pension Service agreed to extend their FX swap facility, capped at $65 billion, through end-2026 (BOK, December 15, 2025). When the pension fund gets dollars through the swap rather than in the spot market, its dollar demand leaves the spot market. Reported reserves fall while the swap is open and come back when it matures. That is why a change in reserves is not, by itself, a measure of intervention. End-October reserves were $443.16 billion (BOK, November 4).

3. FX: where gross flows net out

USD/KRW nets out flows moving in opposite directions. In September, Korea’s current-account surplus was $61.46 billion. In the same month, residents’ overseas portfolio investment rose by $16.63 billion and foreigners’ portfolio investment in Korea fell by $13.81 billion, mainly in equities (BOK September balance of payments briefing). A record trade surplus and a weaker won can both be true at once. The financial account explains the difference.

4. Korea bridge: two foreign books, not one

Foreign equity and foreign bond flows answer to different drivers and do not have to move together. In October, foreigners net sold 25.978 trillion won of listed Korean stocks and net invested 7.375 trillion won in listed bonds. Foreign holdings of listed bonds reached 330.3 trillion won, or 11.3% of the outstanding total (FSS data via Yonhap, November 20).

Part of that bond demand had a mechanical source. FTSE Russell added Korean government bonds to its World Government Bond Index in eight equal monthly steps, from the April 2026 index profiles through the November 2026 profiles (FTSE Russell). With the phase-in done, there is no more monthly index step to explain foreign KTB buying. Any later flow has to be explained by something else, such as hedged yield, reserve-manager allocation or relative value.

Illustration: November 20

Our Korea Close for November 20 shows the two books moving apart in a single session. The KOSPI fell 5.21% to 4,869.96 and foreigners net sold 1,141.1 billion won of KOSPI cash shares. USD/KRW was 1,336.2 at the 15:30 Seoul close, 6.2 won weaker. The 3-year KTB yield fell 7.4 bp to 3.642% and the 10-year fell 8.5 bp to 3.857%. Foreigners bought 12,541 three-year KTB futures contracts. In this framework, that is risk reduction in equities with demand for duration on the other side. It describes one session. It does not say anything about the next one.

How to read a move

  • Rates-led? Did the Fed or BOK path, or the policy-rate gap, change first?
  • Funding-led? Did short-dated swap pricing or US overnight rates move without a rate catalyst?
  • Flow-led? Do KRX and FSS data show foreign selling or buying, and in which book: cash equities, futures or bonds?
  • Structural? Was an index step, a pension-fund hedging operation or a reserves item involved?

A move with no supporting evidence in any of these layers stays unexplained. It does not get a label by default.
