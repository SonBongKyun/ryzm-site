The Borrowed Payout | October 19, 2026

THE BORROWED PAYOUT

In 2025, private equity firms borrowed $72.2 billion through the US leveraged loan market to pay dividends to themselves. That was the second-highest annual total ever recorded, PitchBook-LCD data show, and $50.2 billion of those dividends reached sponsors, the highest annual figure on record.

The mechanism is straightforward. Exits are still blocked. Bain counts 32,000 unsold portfolio companies worth about $3.8 trillion, and distributions to limited partners have run below 15% of net asset value for four consecutive years, an industry record. Cash-starved investors demand liquidity, DPI (distributed to paid-in capital) has replaced IRR as the number that matters, and sponsors who cannot sell have learned to borrow instead. The company takes on the debt and the dividend goes straight to the sponsor. The median gap between buyout and dividend stretched to 4.1 years in 2026.

Lenders were willing. Through June, the average spread on a recap was 322 basis points, the lowest since the financial crisis, and the average yield sat at a four-year low of 7.17%. January’s Ensemble RCM deal, $4.7 billion of debt funding a $1.05 billion dividend, was the fourth-largest such transaction since the start of 2025. July brought $9.11 billion of recap volume, the most since September 2025, with Blackstone’s Carrix and Morton Salt deals cracking the all-time top ten.

Now the valve is closing. Through early September, 2026 recap volume totaled $28.69 billion, down 39% from the same period in 2025, and the average deal shrank to $541 million from $668 million. The Federal Reserve’s recent hike made borrowing pricier. Lenders turned cautious on software issuers after the first quarter’s AI-driven selloff, and new recaps are clustering in AI-insulated sectors like food and manufacturing. Some sponsors are holding off on recaps for companies they hope to sell.

The read: a dividend recap moves leverage without moving ownership. It converts an equity-side liquidity problem into a credit-side leverage problem. The cash leaves, the debt stays, and the eventual buyer inherits both. Spreads compressed while base rates rose, so the pressure sits on borrower coverage, not on lender appetite. If exit markets stay shut into year-end, the pressure to reopen this valve will grow. Watch whether lenders keep saying yes.
