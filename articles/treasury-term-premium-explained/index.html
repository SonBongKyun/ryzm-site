What Is the Treasury Term Premium? Reading a Bond Selloff

RYZM REFERENCE / RATES & BONDS

A higher Treasury yield does not, by itself, mean the market expects a more hawkish Federal Reserve. First separate the expected path of short rates from the price investors place on carrying duration. That distinction changes the questions worth asking after a bond selloff.

In this guide: Definition · Worked example · Model limits · Desk framework · Sources

What is the Treasury term premium?

The term premium is the part of a bond yield associated with compensation for bearing interest-rate risk rather than the expected average short rate over its life. The New York Fed’s ACM framework estimates those components using a five-factor term-structure model and zero-coupon Treasury yields. It is a model decomposition, not a separately traded security. Source: New York Fed methodology.

Fitted yield = expected average short rate + estimated term premium.

The terms must come from a consistent model and maturity. Definitions can differ: the Federal Reserve’s Kim-Wright documentation includes a convexity component within its reported term premium. A fitted zero-coupon yield should not casually be mixed with a quoted coupon-bond yield. Source: Federal Reserve definitions and model caveats.

A worked example: the same selloff, a different explanation

Hypothetical example, not current market data. Suppose a model-fitted 10-year yield rises from 4.00% to 4.25%. Over the same interval, its estimated term premium rises from 0.50% to 0.85%.

At the start, the implied expected-average-short-rate component is 4.00% minus 0.50%, or 3.50%. At the end, it is 4.25% minus 0.85%, or 3.40%. The fitted yield rose 25 basis points even though this expectations component fell 10 basis points. The premium rose 35 basis points.

That arithmetic supports a narrow statement: within this model, the premium component more than accounts for the fitted yield increase. It does not establish which event caused the premium to change. It also does not mean the next meeting is pricing exactly 10 basis points of easing; this is an average over ten years.

The model is not a label for the cause

A close fit to observed yields does not guarantee precise estimates of unobservable expectations. The Federal Reserve explicitly discusses estimation uncertainty, survey inputs and the sensitivity of results to model specification. Compare like-for-like observations and retain the data vintage used in an analysis. Source: Federal Reserve model Q&A.

Nor is the term premium simply a fiscal-risk gauge. Interest-rate uncertainty, the value of bonds as a hedge and shifts in the supply of duration available to investors can matter. Federal Reserve asset purchases are one example of a mechanism that can affect term premiums. These are candidate explanations, not an automatic diagnosis of any particular trading day. Source: Federal Reserve discussion of long-term rates, 2013.

A negative premium is possible. Investors may accept a lower expected return on a long bond when its hedging benefits are valuable. The word premium therefore does not guarantee a positive number. Source: Federal Reserve discussion, 2019.

RYZM desk framework: test the explanation

Our interpretation framework: record the maturity, yield convention, observation dates and source first. Then compare the fitted-yield change with the model’s expectations and premium changes. Do not splice components from different providers to make the arithmetic fit.

Use near-term policy pricing as a cross-check, not as a substitute for the ten-year average. Even OIS forward rates can contain risk premiums; they are not an unfiltered survey of what investors believe. Source: Federal Reserve analysis of policy expectations.

An explanation becomes less persuasive when the dates do not align, the result depends on one model, or a supposedly premium-led move is accompanied by a large repricing of the expected-rate component. Report those tensions instead of smoothing them away. A decomposition earns its place by narrowing the question, not by removing uncertainty.

Two common confusions

Is the 10-year minus 2-year spread the term premium? No. That spread compares two yields. The premium decomposition compares one maturity’s fitted yield with its own expected short-rate component. The two calculations answer different questions.

Does a higher premium identify a buying opportunity? Not on its own. It describes a component of a yield estimate, not a forecast of the investor’s holding-period return. Our use of the measure is diagnostic, not a trade signal.
