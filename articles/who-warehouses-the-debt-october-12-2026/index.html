Who Warehouses the Debt | October 12, 2026

Who Warehouses the Debt

On October 1, the Fed’s supervision chief gave an unusual progress report. The enhanced supplementary leverage ratio, the rule that kept bank dealers from warehousing Treasuries, has been recalibrated. Early results are in.

The mechanics are simple. The eSLR, adopted in 2014, treated a Treasury the same as a risky loan. It was meant as a backstop to risk-based capital rules. It became a binding constraint instead. Banks pulled back from low-return intermediation and chased higher-return risk. The rule meant to protect stability was starving the market that funds the government.

The fix, finalized last November and effective April 1, ties the buffer to each bank’s systemic surcharge instead of a flat 2%. Seven of eight U.S. GSIBs adopted it early. Estimates cited in the speech say the six dealer parents gained nearly $5 trillion of headroom in Q1. Supervisory data show dealer Treasury positions rose from roughly $600 billion to over $700 billion by end-April, concentrated at the banks that had been most constrained.

One dealer told the Fed its leveraged capacity jumped from $1.8 trillion in Q4 2025 to $6.4 trillion after the change. Leveraged exposure across the group rose about $900 billion.

Then the market test arrived. On October 7, Treasury sold $39 billion of 10-year notes at 5.30%, the highest auction yield since 2000. Bid-to-cover hit 2.77, a decade high. Indirect bidders took 80.3%. Primary dealers were left with 2.5%, their smallest share of a 10-year auction since the financial crisis.

Read that carefully. The dealers did not need to warehouse the paper because end investors bought it at price. Bid-ask spreads narrowed, auction-day volatility fell, funding stayed calm. The plumbing worked.

Bowman also flagged the deeper shift. Dealers are buying Treasuries and hedging with short futures, absorbing positions once held by hedge funds in the cash-futures basis trade. That moves duration risk from highly leveraged nonbanks onto bank balance sheets built for it. Less funding-shock fragility, different concentration.

The honest caveat is hers, not mine. The real test is stress. The rule change removes the chance that dealers hit a binding constraint during a risk-off surge, which is exactly when liquidity matters. Until that surge arrives, this is improvement measured in calm weather.

Watch who holds the next wave of supply. Yields tell you the price of duration. Balance sheets tell you who is stuck with it.

Sources and data scope

Source review: 14 October 2026 (KST). This note combines regulatory observations published on 1 October with an auction held on 7 October; it is not a live market report.

Michelle Bowman’s Federal Reserve speech, 1 October 2026, supports the eSLR implementation dates, early adoption, Q1 capacity estimates and Treasury-position observations through April. The capacity figure attributed to one dealer describes GSIB capacity collectively, not that dealer’s own balance sheet. Estimates and market-contact reports retain those qualifications.

Treasury’s 7 October 2026 auction result identifies a 9-year 10-month reopening, $39 billion awarded excluding SOMA, a 5.300% high yield and 2.77 bid-to-cover. The approximately 80.3% indirect-bidder and 2.5% primary-dealer shares use competitive awards as the denominator.

Scope limit: the auction release alone does not establish the historical superlatives in the article. Bowman’s discussion of narrower spreads and calmer funding describes broader market observations; it is not a measurement of that specific auction day. Causal conclusions and the stress-test discussion are analysis.
